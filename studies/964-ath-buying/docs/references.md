# Sources & literature map — Study 964 (All-Time High)

## The claim in the wild

- **"Never catch a falling knife" / "never buy at the top"** — folk advice with no single
  origin, restated in every market cycle. Its testable content is the one measured here:
  that conditional on a record high, forward returns are *worse* than unconditional.
- **Meb Faber, "Are Stock Market Highs a Bearish Signal?"** and the annual crop of
  practitioner notes on buying at records — the standard rebuttal, usually run on the S&P
  alone and on price rather than total-return indices. This study widens the tape set and
  adds the counterfactual the notes usually skip: *where else would the money have gone?*

## Why the answer is not obvious

- **Fama, E. F. (1970), "Efficient Capital Markets: A Review of Theory and Empirical Work",
  *Journal of Finance* 25(2), 383-417.** Under weak-form efficiency the path to today's price
  carries no information about tomorrow's — a record high is just a price.
- **Jegadeesh, N. & Titman, S. (1993), "Returns to Buying Winners and Selling Losers",
  *Journal of Finance* 48(1), 65-91.** The opposite prior: momentum. If prices trend, a
  record high is mildly *good* news, which is the alternative hypothesis here.
- **George, T. J. & Hwang, C.-Y. (2004), "The 52-Week High and Momentum Investing",
  *Journal of Finance* 59(5), 2145-2176.** Proximity to a high as a cross-sectional signal —
  the closest formal literature, though cross-sectional and stock-level rather than
  time-series and index-level.
- **Huddart, S., Lang, M. & Yetman, M. (2009), "Volume and Price Patterns Around a Stock's
  52-Week Highs and Lows", *Management Science* 55(1), 16-31.** Behaviour and volume around
  round-number highs; the anchoring mechanism behind the folklore.
- **Barberis, N. & Thaler, R. (2003), "A Survey of Behavioral Finance", in *Handbook of the
  Economics of Finance*.** Anchoring and reference-point effects — why "the old high" feels
  like a ceiling to a human being.

## The statistics of overlapping forward returns

- **Hansen, L. P. & Hodrick, R. J. (1980), "Forward Exchange Rates as Optimal Predictors of
  Future Spot Rates", *Journal of Political Economy* 88(5), 829-853.** The origin of the
  horizon-lag HAC correction used in every table here.
- **Newey, W. K. & West, K. D. (1987), *Econometrica* 55(3), 703-708.** The estimator itself.
- **Boudoukh, J., Israel, R. & Richardson, M. (2019), "Long-Horizon Predictability: A Cautionary
  Tale", *Financial Analysts Journal* 75(1), 17-48.** Why long-horizon conditional claims are
  far weaker than their *t*-statistics suggest — the reason the non-overlapping cross-check is
  run at all.
- **Valkanov, R. (2003), "Long-Horizon Regressions: Theoretical Results and Applications",
  *Journal of Financial Economics* 68(2), 201-232.** The asymptotics behind that warning.

## Survivorship, the caveat that cannot be fixed here

- **Brown, S. J., Goetzmann, W. N. & Ross, S. A. (1995), "Survival", *Journal of Finance*
  50(3), 853-873.** Why a series that survived to be studied is a biased sample of the
  possible histories.
- **Dimson, E., Marsh, P. & Staunton, M. (2002), *Triumph of the Optimists*.** The
  cross-country record: the US tape used in most record-high studies is the best of the
  sample, not the average of it.

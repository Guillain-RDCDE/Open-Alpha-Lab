# Sources & literature map — Study 982 (The Appetite Gauge)

## The gauge and its ingredients

- **Baker, M., Bradley, B. & Wurgler, J. (2011), "Benchmarks as Limits to Arbitrage:
  Understanding the Low-Volatility Anomaly", *Financial Analysts Journal* 67(1), 40-54.** Why a
  low-volatility half of the index exists as an investable product at all, and why its spread
  against high beta is not a neutral thermometer.
- **Frazzini, A. & Pedersen, L. H. (2014), "Betting Against Beta", *Journal of Financial
  Economics* 111(1), 1-25.** The factor that the high-beta minus low-volatility spread is a
  crude, unhedged version of — and the paper that explains why the hedge matters.
- **Blitz, D. & van Vliet, P. (2007), "The Volatility Effect", *Journal of Portfolio
  Management* 34(1), 102-113.** The other founding low-volatility reference.

## Risk appetite as a predictor

- **Baker, M. & Wurgler, J. (2006), "Investor Sentiment and the Cross-Section of Stock
  Returns", *Journal of Finance* 61(4), 1645-1680.** The canonical sentiment index, several of
  whose components are ratios of speculative to safe assets — the same idea as this gauge.
- **Gonzalez-Hermosillo, B. (2008), "Investors' Risk Appetite and Global Financial Market
  Conditions", IMF Working Paper 08/85.** A survey of the risk-appetite indicators used in
  practice, most of which share this study's confound.
- **Pflueger, C., Siriwardane, E. & Sunderam, A. (2020), "Financial Market Risk Perceptions and
  the Macroeconomy", *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 135(3), 1443-1491.** Builds a
  risk-appetite measure from the *cross-section* of returns explicitly orthogonalised to the
  market — the correct version of what this study tests.

## Getting the inference right

- **Hansen, L. P. & Hodrick, R. J. (1980), *JPE* 88(5), 829-853**, and **Newey & West (1987),
  *Econometrica* 55(3), 703-708.** The overlapping-window standard errors used throughout.
- **Stambaugh, R. F. (1999), "Predictive Regressions", *Journal of Financial Economics* 54(3),
  375-421.** The bias in predictive regressions with persistent regressors — which a trailing
  average certainly is.
- **Welch, I. & Goyal, A. (2008), "A Comprehensive Look at the Empirical Performance of Equity
  Premium Prediction", *Review of Financial Studies* 21(4), 1455-1508.** The reference point
  for how rarely any of these predictors survives out of sample.

# References — Study 200 (ROE-Quality)

## Primary papers

**Asness, C., Frazzini, A., & Pedersen, L. H. (2019).** Quality minus junk.
*Review of Accounting Studies*, 24(1), 34–112.
The canonical academic treatment of quality as a factor. Defines quality along
profitability, growth, safety, and payout axes; ROE is a key profitability component.
Shows quality minus junk (QMJ) has significant returns globally, but noting that a
large portion is explained by the value/growth tilt rather than pure quality.

**Novy-Marx, R. (2013).** The other side of value: the gross profitability premium.
*Journal of Financial Economics*, 108(1), 1–28.
Argues gross profit / total assets (not net income / equity) is the cleanest
profitability signal, being harder to manipulate and closer to "economic profit".
This study uses GP/Assets as the head-to-head comparison for ROE.

**Fama, E. F., & French, K. R. (2015).** A five-factor asset pricing model.
*Journal of Financial Economics*, 116(1), 1–22.
Adds profitability (RMW) and investment (CMA) factors to the three-factor model.
The RMW factor is constructed from operating profitability (not ROE), reflecting
Novy-Marx's critique of net-income-based measures.

**Fama, E. F., & French, K. R. (2006).** Profitability, investment and average returns.
*Journal of Financial Economics*, 82(3), 491–518.
Earlier evidence that firms with high profitability earn higher returns, underpinning
the RMW factor and the broad "quality" narrative.

## Related work

**Graham, B., & Dodd, D. (1934).** *Security Analysis*. McGraw-Hill.
The intellectual origin of ROE as a quality proxy: firms that consistently earn high
returns on equity were considered to possess a "franchise" or durable competitive
advantage. This study tests whether that old intuition survives in modern price data.

**Haugen, R. A., & Baker, N. L. (1996).** Commonality in the determinants of expected
stock returns. *Journal of Financial Economics*, 41(3), 401–439.
Early empirical evidence linking high-quality (high-profitability) characteristics to
higher forward returns; a pre-factor-model precursor to QMJ.

**Asness, C., Frazzini, A., Israel, R., Moskowitz, T., & Pedersen, L. (2015).**
Fact, fiction, and value investing. *Journal of Portfolio Management*, 42(1), 34–52.
Clarifies the interaction between value and quality: high-ROE firms tend to trade at
premium valuations, so the forward return depends critically on whether quality is
"priced in". On the S&P 500 universe, which selects for high-quality survivors, the
quality premium is likely already embedded in prices.

**McLean, R. D., & Pontiff, J. (2016).** Does academic publication of anomalies destroy
stock return predictability? *Journal of Finance*, 71(1), 5–32.
Finds that many published anomalies decay post-publication as arbitrage capital
eliminates them. The quality/ROE premium, widely known since the early 2000s, is a
plausible candidate for publication-induced decay.

## Survivorship bias

**Kothari, S. P., Sabino, J. S., & Zach, T. (2005).** Implications of survival and
data trimming for tests of market efficiency. *Journal of Accounting and Economics*,
39(1), 129–161.
Documents that survivorship bias inflates apparent anomaly returns in studies using
only the current index members. This study explicitly labels all results as
"upper bounds" due to the survivorship-biased EDGAR cache.

**Shumway, T. (1997).** The delisting bias in CRSP data. *Journal of Finance*, 52(1),
327–340.
Demonstrates that excluding delisted firms biases returns upwards, particularly for
small and financially distressed companies. The distressed (low-ROE) firms most likely
to be excluded are precisely the ones the quality short leg would target, so the
measured "inverse quality premium" here is mechanically an upper bound.

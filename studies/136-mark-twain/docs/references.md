# References -- Study 136 (Mark-Twain)

## Primary source

**Mark Twain (Samuel Clemens).** *Pudd'nhead Wilson and Those Extraordinary Twins* (1894).
Chapter 13 contains the original calendar aphorism: "October. This is one of the peculiarly
dangerous months to speculate in stocks. The others are July, January, September, April, November,
May, March, June, December, August, and February." Twain was satirising speculation, not issuing
a calendar forecast.

## Academic literature on calendar effects in equities

**Rozeff, M.S. and Kinney, W.R. (1976).** "Capital market seasonality: The case of stock
returns." *Journal of Financial Economics*, 3(4), 379-402. Early documentation of the January
effect; notes that no single month reliably underperforms across specifications.

**French, K.R. (1980).** "Stock returns and the weekend effect." *Journal of Financial Economics*,
8(1), 55-69. Seminal calendar-effect paper; establishes that calendar anomalies require careful
inference before trading implications can be drawn.

**Bouman, S. and Jacobsen, B. (2002).** "The Halloween Indicator, 'Sell in May and Go Away':
Another Puzzle." *American Economic Review*, 92(5), 1618-1635. The paper that popularised the
sell-in-May / Halloween indicator; provides the cleanest evidence that winter months outperform
summer months -- but explicitly *not* October vs the rest.

**Jacobsen, B. and Zhang, C.Y. (2018).** "The Halloween Indicator, 'Sell in May and Go Away':
Everywhere and all the Time." Available at SSRN. Extends Bouman-Jacobsen to 114 countries and
a 300-year UK equity series; finds the winter premium is persistent but small.

**McLean, R.D. and Pontiff, J. (2016).** "Does Academic Research Destroy Stock Return
Predictability?" *Journal of Finance*, 71(1), 5-32. Documents post-publication decay in
anomaly returns; calendar effects are among those that fade after academic attention.

**Kamstra, M.J., Kramer, L.A., and Levi, M.D. (2003).** "Winter Blues: A SAD Stock Market
Cycle." *American Economic Review*, 93(1), 324-343. Proposes seasonal affective disorder as a
channel for winter-premium effects; one of few papers that attempts a causal mechanism for
seasonal returns.

## On inference for calendar effects

**Lo, A.W. (2002).** "The Statistics of Sharpe Ratios." *Financial Analysts Journal*, 58(4),
36-52. Documents autocorrelation and fat tails as sources of inflation in conventional Sharpe
inference; the HAC t-stat used in this study follows Newey and West (1987).

**Newey, W.K. and West, K.D. (1987).** "A Simple, Positive Semi-Definite, Heteroskedasticity
and Autocorrelation Consistent Covariance Matrix." *Econometrica*, 55(3), 703-708. The
estimator behind the HAC t-stats reported in this study.

## On survivorship-of-anecdotes bias

**Tversky, A. and Kahneman, D. (1973).** "Availability: A heuristic for judging frequency and
probability." *Cognitive Psychology*, 5(2), 207-232. The foundational paper on availability
bias -- the mechanism by which three memorable October crashes dominate decade-long base rates
in practitioners' mental models.
